Hi! I'm Chantal D'Amore

Postdoctoral Researcher in Social Psychology

I am a Social Psychologist (PhD) and postdoctoral researcher at the University of Amsterdam, where I study the social-emotional dynamics of normative decline in collaboration with Gerben van Kleef.

My broader research interests lie at the intersection of political psychology and social psychology: polarization, moralization, norms, values, emotions, and social identities. I use experimental, longitudinal, and social interaction designs.

Here you can find more about my research, publications, and data & materials.

Research themes & projects

Polarization and Moralization Project

In my PhD project, I have conducted research on political polarization and morality. This includes modern terms such as "culture wars," "echo chambers," and "identity politics." Central to this research is the question of how and when people develop moralized beliefs about polarized topics — that is, the psychological process in which an opinion transforms into a fundamental belief of "good" versus "evil." This applies, for example, to topics such as climate change, immigration/refugees, and equal rights for various (groups of) people. In addition, I zoom in on the phenomenon of polarization and examine the consequences of polarization for the individual and for society as a whole: Not all polarization is created equal. While rising political disagreement is often portrayed as a threat to democracy, this chapter shows that the reality is more nuanced—and more hopeful. Using a systematic review of 173 empirical effects across 57 studies, the Polarization Triangle Framework reveals that polarization only becomes truly dangerous when three ingredients converge at once: people disagree on the issues, those disagreements are rooted in clashing moral values, and those moral-opinion divides are aligned with partisan identities. This toxic combination—called Structural Moral Opinion (SMO) polarization—is what predicts support for political violence, authoritarian leaders, and the erosion of democratic norms. Without the moral and structural ingredients, rising polarization actually motivates constructive political engagement. The takeaway? Societies should not fear disagreement—they should fear the moralization and structural entrenchment of it.
